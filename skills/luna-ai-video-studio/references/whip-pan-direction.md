# Whip-pan direction

Use this reference when a whip-pan may improve a reaction, reveal, pursuit, or transition. Consider it automatically during camera planning; use it only when it serves a specific story beat. Apply across general Luna work, the expanded v2 workflow, sitcom, action, and Fia & Theo mode. Preserve each project's camera, character, sound, and approval constraints.

## Choose the mode before writing

- Continuous-take whip: rotate the camera through the same physical space, with a feasible start position, sweep, and landing. Preserve elapsed time, geography, and motion. A pan alone does not move the camera to the opposite side of a bench or change its physical distance from a face.
- Edited whip transition: conceal a cut inside directional motion blur, matching outgoing and incoming screen motion, apparent speed, exposure, and composition. Use only when cuts are permitted. Never describe this as an uninterrupted take.
- Honor locked-off, no-cut, restrained-camera, and approved-story constraints. Do not add a whip simply to increase shot count. There is no required number of whips per 30 seconds.

## Select a motivated moment

Use a pointing gesture, gaze shift, sound cue, revealed obstacle, sudden escape, or completed line as the reason to redirect attention. Decide what new information the landing adds.

- Sitcom: land on the listener's readable reaction or reply. Preserve exact dialogue, response timing, and room geography.
- Fia & Theo: use for jealousy, a pursuer's reaction, or an already established obstacle. Preserve anatomical markings, scale, pendants, feline behavior, and the active episode's audio rules. Keep the route of a zigzag chase understandable.
- Action: reveal a new threat or follow attention after a readable impact. Keep attack, contact, force transfer, and response visible.
- Drama, romance, horror, or product work: omit the whip when it would disrupt emotional attention, suspense, or material detail.

Do not cover the first-second event, a trip, collision, prop release, touch, or essential expression with blur. Show the cause clearly before redirecting attention. A motivated sound-led reveal may keep its source offscreen until the planned reveal.

## Specify the move and landing

Write only the relevant controls:
1. Trigger and starting subject or framing.
2. Camera position and side, sweep direction, and destination's established location.
3. Landing subject, shot size, height or angle, and eyeline.
4. Brief directional blur during travel; decelerate and regain a readable, stable image on arrival.
5. The reaction or new information visible after landing, with enough time to register.
6. Continuing action, prop ownership, light, and screen direction across the move.

Use physical staging to obtain the requested shot size. If a close landing requires travel, allow time for that travel; otherwise choose a feasible medium shot. Do not disguise a spatial jump as a pan. Preserve the action axis unless a visible camera path deliberately establishes the change.

For open spaces, anchor the destination to a fixed landmark or established event location. Use the known splash point, doorway, bench end, or branch rather than an unspecified sea, street, or sky.

## Timing and sound

Budget anticipation when needed, sweep, settling, performance, dialogue, and aftermath inside the beat. Never treat the reaction as taking no time. If the beat is crowded, shorten or remove the move before compressing the joke or dialogue.

Use a brief whoosh only when it fits the requested sound style. Do not add one to every whip by default. Preserve dialogue clarity, continuing ambience, music preferences, and bans on human voices. Camera instructions do not authorize changing the soundtrack.

For chase scenes, keep subject speed and apparent camera tracking speed continuous before and after the sweep; do not insert an unintended slow-motion hold. Settle into tracking when the subject remains in motion.

## Connected clips

Carry forward character positions, eyelines, hand/paw and prop states, dirt or wetness, light direction, action phase, subject direction and speed, camera direction and speed, and continuing audio. Use an accepted boundary frame as conditioning only when available and supported. Text handoff alone is not proof of a seamless join.

Inspect actual boundary frames, motion, exposure, sound perspective, and decay tails before approving a join. Do not split at 30 seconds as a universal rule; use the approved duration and verified provider limits.

## Compact prompt pattern

"At [visible trigger], from [camera position and starting framing], whip-pan [direction] to [established destination], landing in a [feasible shot size] of [subject]. Brief directional motion blur during the sweep; settle into [stable framing or matching-speed tracking]. Show [specific reaction] clearly before the next beat. Maintain [essential continuity states]."

For an edited transition, explicitly state that a cut is hidden in matching motion blur. For a continuous take, explicitly retain the physical path without a cut. Adapt the pattern; never copy unused placeholders into a final prompt.

## Pre-generation and output checks

- Does the move add a reaction, reveal, or useful change of attention?
- Does the selected mode honor the user's camera constraints?
- Can the camera reach the destination and shot size without teleporting?
- Are trigger, direction, destination, and landing performance unambiguous?
- Are hook, contact, prop transfer, and reaction readable?
- Does the allocated time include settling and performance?
- Do geography, identity, accessories, light, and ongoing movement survive?
- Does sound respect the scene's existing rules?

If any check fails, simplify or omit the move. When reviewing generated footage, inspect the landing as well as the blur; reject swapped faces, changed positions, duplicated props, or frozen reactions. Blur does not repair continuity errors. Distinguish written prompt checks from inspected output and do not promise a higher success rate without generated evidence.
