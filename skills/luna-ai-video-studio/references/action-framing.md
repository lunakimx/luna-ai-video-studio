# Action Hook Framing and Impact Tracking

Read this reference for action, combat, confrontation, or fantasy-action planning, storyboards, image briefs, final prompts, and reviews. Apply it with the existing first-second hook, reference fidelity, fantasy physics, and mandatory pre-generation QC rules.

These are directing choices, not fixed model controls or evidence that a video will reach a particular view count. Do not add action or fantasy to an unrelated scene. The latest explicit user framing, camera, reference, and output restrictions take priority.

## 1. Event-led opening close-ups

Consider a close-up of an eye, face, hand, weapon, imminent contact, or supernatural trigger when it makes the opening event readable within the first second, including with sound off.

A close-up must reveal an event, threat, or change. A beautiful static portrait alone does not pass the viral-first hook gate.

Choose the shot size that communicates the event best. A centered full moon, looming silhouette, or wide human-versus-giant scale reveal can be stronger for the specific story. Do not force a close-up when it hides the threat, loses necessary scale, contradicts an approved first frame, or reveals a creature the user wants concealed.

If starting tight, give the next beat enough spatial information to understand the attacker, target, and direction of force. In a continuous take, reveal that information through a feasible camera move or the action entering the existing composition, not an implied cut.

First-frame conditioning is a separate constraint: when an exact supplied starting frame conflicts with a requested close-up, identify the mismatch and propose a supported crop or reference revision. Do not promise that prompt wording alone overrides fixed input framing. Do not modify the image without authorization.

## 2. Readable composition without a mandatory grid

Rule of thirds means a composition guide formed by two horizontal and two vertical divisions, not a three-panel split screen and not a requirement to show exactly three subjects.

Use thirds, asymmetry, or directional negative space when they help distinguish subject, threat, and attack path. Preserve approved positions rather than moving a character merely to satisfy a grid.

Useful options include:
- placing the hero and threat on opposing sides with space for the approaching force;
- keeping an expressive face and the visible power source in separate readable areas;
- keeping a small hero, giant limb, and a familiar scale anchor legible together;
- reserving lead room for an incoming strike or escape.

Centered or symmetrical framing remains valid for a frontal threat, iconic full moon, direct impact, or ceremonial presence. Judge the action's legibility, not compliance with thirds. Never score a centered image as weak solely because it is centered.

Do not crop away the contact point, the relevant limb, or the target's reaction just to maintain a preferred composition. In vertical shots, preserve enough width for the attack path and enough height for scale and body response.

## 3. Impact-tracking camera fallback

When elaborate camera choreography obscures combat, simplify around:

ATTACK SOURCE → STRIKE PATH → CONTACT OR BLOCK → FORCE TRANSFER → TARGET RESPONSE

Choose a readable medium or wider three-quarter view when source and target must share the image. Use one modest pan or lateral tracking move along the attack line, then settle sufficiently around contact for the hit and recoil to register. Continue to the aftermath only after the target response reads.

Tracking the impact point is an attention strategy. It does not mean attaching the camera to a fist, making the lens collide with the target, or whipping between every limb. Camera travel, framing, and focus are separate decisions. Do not force a rack focus when both participants must remain readable.

If the user locks the camera, keep it locked and stage source, contact, and reaction within that composition. The fallback must not silently override a fixed-camera request.

Preserve screen direction and the established camera side. Any intentional axis crossing must be shown continuously with enough landmarks and spatial information to stay understandable. Do not conceal a camera teleport behind a pillar or a full-frame impact flash.

Contact can be a strike, block, near miss, or deflection according to the script. The response must match the event: do not create a hit reaction when the attack missed. For a hit, show force transfer into the target's posture, balance, motion, or visible environmental response.

Do not require extra shake, an orbit, zoom, impact flash, slow motion, and speed ramp in the same short beat. Keep the camera simpler when subject motion or supernatural effects are already complex. A stable readable view is an acceptable fallback.

## 4. Fantasy and giant-opponent integration

Preserve the Fantasy Action Physics Gate. Supernatural motion may exceed ordinary physics, while source, trajectory, destination, and aftermath remain traceable.

Anchor portals and energy sources in the scene, not to a fixed screen corner during camera movement. Their apparent screen position changes consistently with framing and perspective. An energy trail starts from the actual source and reaches the actual contact point; it must not restart from another position after a camera move.

For a small hero versus a giant, maintain at least one readable scale relationship whenever scale carries the event. Track a specific attack or contact region without losing the hero and the giant's responding body part. An extreme close-up may provide a hook, but must not replace every view needed to understand the confrontation.

Visual effects, bloom, particles, and motion blur must not hide the strike or the target response. If audio is allowed, synchronize impact sound to visible contact and preserve any specified animal-only vocal rules. Audio emphasis cannot substitute for a missing visual reaction.

## 5. Automatic action-framing QC

Before delivering an action storyboard, image brief, or final prompt, check:

1. Does the opening contain a readable event within the first second, and is its shot size compatible with the actual input reference?
2. Can the viewer locate the subject, threat, attack direction, and any necessary scale anchor without a compulsory thirds or close-up rule?
3. Are the strike path, contact or miss, force transfer, and immediate response understandable, without effects hiding them?
4. Is the camera physically and temporally coherent, preserving any fixed-camera instruction and the intended continuous take?
5. Would a smaller camera move or stable view improve the beat, and are visual and audio impact timings aligned when audio is used?

Repair failed items before presenting a final prompt. For a generated video, judge only accessible footage and audio; mark uninspected evidence as unverified. Written QC does not establish generated-video quality, model compliance, audience retention, or a guaranteed view count.
