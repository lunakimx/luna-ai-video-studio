## Episode development

Recover the accepted state, then choose one immediate visual event for 0.0-1.0 seconds. Match the actual first-frame input. Close-ups, two-shots, centered framing, and thirds are options, not compulsory formats. A strong expression still needs a visible cause or unresolved situation; do not spend the opening on a neutral pose.

Write a brief cause-and-effect beat sheet: character desire, visible trigger, action, complication, reaction, payoff. Keep one dominant action and one readable response per short beat when complexity is risky. Protect enough time for a reaction and ending to register; do not demand five slow reveals in the final two seconds. Do not turn every scene into a chase or reset an approved calm opening.

Use emotional patterns as optional proposals: rival contact noticed, protective interception, escape interrupted, food lost through a visible mistake, failed comfort attempt followed by mutual cheek rubbing. These are storytelling choices, not diagnoses of real feline emotions. No default baby reveal or surprise extra cast.

## Image roles and necessity

Separate four jobs:
- identity reference: what each cat looks like;
- environment reference: doors, windows, furniture, ledges, landmarks, lighting;
- motion keyframe: a selected before-contact, contact, or resolved state;
- review storyboard: a human-readable planning board, not automatically a model input.

There is no fixed image quota. For each proposed image, identify which likely failure it prevents, whether an existing approved asset does the job, and what changes if it is removed. Merge redundant beats; retain an intermediate image only when it materially clarifies movement, prop ownership, or a difficult state. Never claim that six sequential uploads are a universal provider requirement.

Check the actual provider's supported reference roles, ordering, first/last-frame controls, and limits before relying on them. Do not put a character sheet first by habit. A multipanel board may be planning-only. Single-character views and a duo size reference can be useful, but do not generate all of them automatically. If exact first-frame conditioning conflicts with the desired hook crop, flag or request the authorized reference revision before generation.

## Motion-ready image brief

For each required image specify: asset ID and role, active cast, one visible action or preparation, emotion trigger, each cat's position and facing, relevant paws and tails, prop owner and attachment, environment landmarks, camera side and height, lighting, next movement, and acceptance checks. Keep unchanged details sourced from the bible rather than inventing a new description each time.

Prefer a pre-action state when the movement itself must be visible: grounded crouch before a jump, mouth holding the fish before release, slightly separated faces before one grooming contact. A contact or after-state reference can still be useful if its role is explicitly assigned. Do not inadvertently require a dropped fish to begin on the floor or a jump to begin after landing.

Use uncluttered full-body identity views when needed for paw or tail checks. Do not force all paws into close-ups. A portrait is not proof that hidden markings passed QC. Keep the two cats visually separable around contact; avoid identical overlapping silhouettes, tongue intersections, or a tail whose attachment cannot be traced.

Maintain coat color under changing light, contact shadows under load-bearing paws, and sensible room for the next motion. Do not add captions, grids, arrows, color chips, or labels to video input frames unless requested. Preserve the user-approved animation style; an unrelated IRL or motion-transfer experiment must not silently replace the animation baseline.
